Project · Build it, defend it

A first customer, and your defense in front of me

Your group now runs a small cloud company, and you're about to sign your first real customer.

A customer walks in with a real need and one annoying constraint.
You adapt your Puff to them, then pitch it like a cloud provider pitches a client, and I play the customer.

🎯 By the end of the project

  • You can turn a customer's need into concrete changes on your Puff.
  • You can explain every box of your cloud, including the ones a teammate built.
  • You can put a monthly price on your design and an honest promise on its uptime and recovery.
  • You can demo a working cloud live and handle questions you didn't prepare for.

📖 Words you'll need
  • Customer (or tenant): the person or team whose app runs on your cloud.
  • Onboarding: everything you do to welcome a new customer onto your cloud.
  • Base Puff: the one member's Puff your group builds on and demos from.
  • Twist: your customer's special constraint, the one that forces a design change.
  • Uptime: the share of time your service answers. 99.9% allows about 43 minutes of downtime a month.
  • Recovery promise: how long a restore takes after a disaster, and how much recent data could be lost. Engineers call these RTO and RPO (Recovery Time and Recovery Point Objective).
  • AZ (Availability Zone): one data center site inside an AWS region, with its own power and network.
  • TTL (Time To Live): how long a cache keeps a copy before asking the original again.
  • Scheduled scaling: telling Auto Scaling "add servers at 19:50 on Friday" instead of waiting for the CPU to climb.
  • Personal data: anything that identifies a real person. In Europe, the GDPR (General Data Protection Regulation) sets the rules for it.

🏪 Your group is a small cloud provider

For six courses you built Puff, your own small cloud on top of AWS, and hosted one tenant on it, the board app.
Here's what you have:

  • file storage (S3)
  • servers and a database
  • a private network with one public door
  • secrets and alarms
  • extra servers when traffic grows (Auto Scaling)
  • a template that rebuilds it all (CloudFormation)

A real customer doesn't care that you used a VPC (Virtual Private Cloud, your private network inside AWS). They ask four things:

  1. Will it be up when I need it?
  2. What happens when something breaks?
  3. What does it cost me each month?
  4. Is my data safe?

Your project answers those with a working Puff, a diagram and a live defense.
Companies do this every day with internal platform teams, you're doing it at sandbox size.

🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Your team and your base Puff

Groups of 3 form.
You don't choose your group and you don't know yet whose Puff becomes the base, so make sure yours from course 5 works.

Your group picks one member's Puff as the base, and everything you demo runs in that sandbox.
Pick the one that works best.

  • The other two test risky changes in their own sandboxes first, then apply them to the base.
  • Split the work however you like, but each of you will be asked about a part you didn't build.
⚠️

The sandbox suspends any account running more than 9 EC2 instances (EC2 is AWS's virtual server service), and a suspended account means no demo.

Before scaling anything in the base account, count what's running (an old puff-api from course 2, Cloud9 environments, test instances) and terminate what you don't need.
Keep puff-asg at max 4.

The evening before course 6, run check.sh on the base Puff (see the pre-flight below) and fix what fails.

📋 Pick your customer

Every group has the same mission, onboard one customer onto your Puff, adapt it to them, and defend your offer.
At most 3 groups per customer, first come first served, so every customer gets someone.

Each customer runs a version of the board app and brings one twist that forces a real design decision.

1. The student association's gala ticket queue. 400 tickets for the winter gala. Sales open Friday at 20:00 sharp and sell out in about 6 minutes. Each post is a ticket request with a name and a student ID.

  • Twist: all the traffic lands in a few minutes you know in advance. Scaling on CPU reacts after the rush starts, so how are the servers ready before 20:00?

2. The conference's live Q&A board. A 600-seat conference shows live questions on the big screen and on every phone in the room. A few dozen people post, everyone else refreshes every few seconds.

  • Twist: about 100 reads for every post. Do all those reads need to reach the database?

3. The campus lost and found. Finders post a short description and a photo, owners browse to find their stuff. A few hundred posts a month.

  • Twist: photo uploads. Where do photos live, how do they get there without passing through your servers, and how does the board learn a new one arrived?

4. The food truck's pre-orders. A food truck in front of the school takes pre-orders from 11:00 to 13:30 on weekdays. Outside that window, nobody uses it.

  • Twist: the platform sits idle about 21 hours a day and all weekend. What does your course 5 Puff cost this customer per month, and what can you switch off without breaking the lunch rush?

5. The hospital's volunteer shifts. About 80 volunteers at a Paris hospital sign up for shifts: name, phone number, slot.

  • Twist: personal data of people in France that must stay in the EU.
  • Your sandbox only works in us-east-1 (Virginia), so build it there.
  • Then explain what changes for the real customer: the region, what the CDN may cache, encryption, and who can read the data.

6. The small software company's status page. A 10-person SaaS (Software as a Service, software you rent online) company needs a public page where the engineer on duty posts incident updates and customers check whether things are broken.

  • Twist: a status page has one job, staying up when everything else is down. What still answers if your API, your database or your whole Auto Scaling group is gone?
💡

The twist is the main part of the project.

Your course 5 Puff with a new title on the HTML page hasn't onboarded anyone.
You need one real change that handles the twist, and you explain it against the other options.

check.sh tests exactly these 9 lines, nothing hidden. You get it in course 5, run it as often as you want.

The website and the board app take two different roads.

The website goes from the browser to CloudFront, which keeps copies near your users and gets the files from your S3 bucket, so no server is involved.
The board app goes through the load balancer, the only public door, to a server in one of two private subnets, one per AZ.
Only those servers can reach the database, which sits in one AZ in your sandbox.

If a server dies, the load balancer sends traffic to the healthy one and Auto Scaling starts a new one.
A CloudWatch alarm watches a number, like the load balancer's errors, and when it trips it tells the puff-alerts topic, which emails you.

Key point: if something here is missing, say so in your defense.
A gap you name is a decision. A gap I find in the console is a surprise, and customers hate surprises.

⭐ Extras that make your offer stand out

Do these only once the package works.
One extra done well beats three half-done.
Extras earn no points on their own, they make your twist and your answers stronger.

  • A Lambda function triggered by an event, like your course 5 upload trigger, for example a new lost and found photo posts a "new item" message.
  • CloudFront caching rules chosen on purpose:
  • a long TTL for the website files
  • a separate rule for /api/* that caches GET /api/messages for a few seconds or caches nothing, depending on your twist
  • A second alarm on what your customer cares about, like unhealthy servers behind the ALB.
  • A monthly cost estimate from the AWS Pricing Calculator, link or screenshot attached.
  • A disaster recovery plan in 5 lines: what you back up, where the copy lives, how long a restore takes, how much data the customer could lose, who presses the button.

🎤 The defense: pitch it like a cloud provider

Your slot is 8 minutes, timed, and the next group plugs in while you answer questions.
I stop you at 2:30 even mid-sentence, so rehearse once with a timer.

TimeWhat
2 min 30Your pitch. All three speak
2 minBreak-fix: I break something, one of you fixes it
3 minOne question each, about a minute per person
30 sSwap

During the questions I'm your customer, asking what happens if this breaks, why it costs that much and who sees my data.

A pitch that works, built on the three words the customer cares about:

  • 20 seconds: your customer and their twist, with numbers (how many users, when, what data).
  • 1 minute: the diagram, following one request from the browser to the database and back.
  • 1 minute: the live demo, ending on the proof you handled the twist.
  • 10 seconds each: how your Puff scales, how it stays available when one AZ goes, and how it recovers after a disaster.

Before and during your slot:

  • Open every tab before your turn: CloudFront URL, ALB address, target group, RDS, alarms, CloudFormation stack.
  • Check the sandbox timer, a lab session expiring mid-demo is avoidable.
  • Show the twist, don't describe it. If your gala answer is scheduled scaling, open EC2 > Auto Scaling groups > puff-asg > Automatic scaling.
  • Say "I don't know" when you don't, then say how you'd find out. Clients trust a provider who admits a gap more than one who bluffs.

🧪 Hands-on: pre-flight check before your defense

You do this in the sandbox, in the base member's account.
Run check.sh at the freeze and post its output, then run the commands below in the browser terminal 10 minutes before your slot, so nothing in the console surprises you during the pitch.

0. Run the project check (I run the same script at your slot):

curl -sO https://ricoleabricot.github.io/puff/project/check.sh
bash check.sh <base-member-login>

It prints PASS or FAIL for each of the 9 items, with the reason, and your score out of 6. It only reads, it never changes anything.

  1. Count running instances (must be 9 or fewer, comfortably fewer):
aws ec2 describe-instances \
  --filters Name=instance-state-name,Values=pending,running \
  --query 'length(Reservations[].Instances[])'
  1. Confirm the database isn't public:
aws rds describe-db-instances --db-instance-identifier puff-db \
  --query 'DBInstances[0].PubliclyAccessible'
  1. Confirm the ASG spans 2 AZs:
aws autoscaling describe-auto-scaling-groups --auto-scaling-group-names puff-asg \
  --query 'AutoScalingGroups[0].[AvailabilityZones,MinSize,MaxSize,length(Instances)]'
  1. Check the alarms and the CloudFormation stacks:
aws cloudwatch describe-alarms --query 'MetricAlarms[].[AlarmName,StateValue]' --output table
aws cloudformation describe-stacks --query 'Stacks[].[StackName,StackStatus]' --output table
  1. Hit the API through the load balancer and list what carries your tags:
ALB_DNS=$(aws elbv2 describe-load-balancers --names puff-alb \
  --query 'LoadBalancers[0].DNSName' --output text)

curl -s "http://$ALB_DNS/health"
aws resourcegroupstaggingapi get-resources --tag-filters Key=Project,Values=puff \
  --query 'ResourceTagMappingList[].ResourceARN' --output table
✅

How you know it works:

  • the instance count is 9 or less
  • step 2 prints false
  • step 3 shows two AZs (for example us-east-1a and us-east-1b)
  • your alarms read OK, your stack reads CREATE_COMPLETE or UPDATE_COMPLETE
  • curl returns {"status":"ok"}
  • the tag list includes your ALB, ASG, RDS instance and bucket
🧯 If it doesn't work
  • curl hangs: check the target group has healthy targets in EC2 > Target groups, then check puff-alb-sg allows port 80 from 0.0.0.0/0 and puff-api-sg allows 8080 from puff-alb-sg.
  • An alarm sits in INSUFFICIENT_DATA: normal for some metrics on a quiet Puff. Be ready to explain why.
  • Stack in UPDATE_ROLLBACK_COMPLETE: your last change failed and AWS undid it, so the stack still works. Read the Events tab to say what failed.
  • The sandbox session expired: restart it, wait for the green dot, run the checks again.

🧯 Survival tips

  • The sandbox closes on November 25, the day of the defenses. No "I'll fix it tonight": whatever runs at the freeze is what you demo.
  • If the sandbox fails and it's not your fault (AWS Academy down, lab outage, Wi-Fi), I grade the package from the check.sh output you posted at the freeze, you explain the twist on your diagram, and the break-fix becomes a "here's the symptom, where do you look" question. Going over 9 instances is on you.
  • Get the customer's case working end to end early, then polish. A half-built twist is worse than a clear plan.
  • Delete what you don't use, leftover instances push you toward the 9-instance limit and make your diagram look wrong.

Your diagram is on screen during the pitch, nothing to hand in.
Any diagram tool works (draw.io, Excalidraw, a readable whiteboard photo), as long as it shows:

  • the VPC, the subnets and their AZs
  • every service
  • the arrows a request follows

🤔 Think about it

  • Your customer is the gala. A teammate wants puff-asg min at 4 all week "to be safe". What does that cost compared with your plan, and is there a case where they're right?
  • For the conference board, you cache GET /api/messages in CloudFront for 5 seconds. A speaker answers a question on stage and the screen still shows it as open. Bug or choice, and how do you pick the number of seconds?
  • The status page customer's API, database and ASG are all down. What can their users still see, and which piece of your Puff made that possible?
  • In real life the hospital customer runs in eu-west-3 (Paris), with CloudFront in front. Could personal data leave the EU, and how would you check?
  • You say "the database breaks first at 10 times the traffic". What would you look at in CloudWatch to prove it?
💡 Hints
  • Price both options for a month in the Pricing Calculator: 4 servers all the time against 2 servers plus 2 more for one hour on Fridays.
  • Who suffers from 5 seconds of old data, and who suffers when 600 phones hit the database every 3 seconds? The cache time is the price of protecting the database.
  • What does CloudFront serve straight from S3, without the API or the database?
  • CloudFront caches only what you allow. Look at cache rules per path and the Cache-Control headers your API sends.
  • Compare RDS CPUUtilization, DatabaseConnections and ReadIOPS with the ALB's TargetResponseTime. Slow responses with calm server CPU point behind the servers.
