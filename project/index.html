Project · Build it, defend it

Your platform, your idea, and your defense in front of me

Your group runs a small platform, built course after course, and you defend it on the last day.

Follow every course and finish every milestone, and your platform works: that alone is 10/20.
On top, you adapt it to one small idea from school life, and you defend it in 8 minutes. Everything happens during the course hours.

🎯 By the end of the project

  • You can turn a real need into concrete changes on your platform.
  • You can explain every box of your platform, including the ones a teammate built, and rebuild it all from your repo.
  • You can put a monthly price on your design and an honest promise on its uptime and recovery.
  • You can demo a working cloud live and handle questions you didn't prepare for.

🏪 Your group is a small platform team

For six courses you built your own small platform, around an app from GitHub.
Here's what you have:

  • file storage (S3)
  • containers and a database
  • a private network with one public door
  • secrets and alarms
  • extra containers when traffic grows (your own autoscaler)
  • a template that rebuilds it all (CloudFormation)

Whoever uses a platform doesn't care that you used a VPC (Virtual Private Cloud, your private network inside AWS). They ask four things:

  1. Will it be up when I need it?
  2. What happens when something breaks?
  3. What would it cost each month on real AWS?
  4. Is my data safe?

Your defense answers those with a working platform, a diagram and a short demo.

🧑‍🤝‍🧑 Your team and your repo

Groups of 3, formed in course 2. Your repo is the shared truth, not a laptop: infra/deploy.sh (then your CloudFormation templates) must rebuild your whole platform from zero on any member's machine, laptop or Codespace.
Each member runs Floci on their own machine, so each can break, wipe and rebuild without blocking the others.

📋 Pick your idea

Every group picks one small idea from school life at EPITA Paris and adapts its platform to it. No new app to write: your app from GitHub stays the same, the idea changes how your cloud around it behaves.
At most 6 groups per idea, first come first served.

1. The midnight deadline. A project is due at 23:42. Nobody submits at 20:00, everybody submits between 23:20 and 23:42, and the submission page must not fall over in the last 10 minutes.

  • What it changes: the rush is known in advance, and scaling on load reacts after it has started. How are your containers ready at 23:20?
  • Pieces you already have: your autoscaler from M4, plus EventBridge Scheduler calling it before the rush.

2. The live room board. A page shows which machine rooms on campus have free seats. A handful of people update it, hundreds of students refresh it every few seconds from their phones between two classes.

  • What it changes: about 100 reads for every write. Do all those reads need to reach your containers and your database?
  • Pieces you already have: CloudFront from M4, this time in front of your load balancer too, with a cache time you choose and defend.

3. The internship CV drop. Students upload their CV for internships and alternance, and only the internship office may read them.

  • What it changes: personal data. Where do the files live, who can read them, and how do you prove nobody else can?
  • Pieces you already have: a private S3 bucket (not your public status page), a least-privilege role like your deployer from M3, and Floci accepts eu-west-3 (Paris) if you want the data in France.
💡

The idea is a small part of the grade, and it happens in class.

You design it in course 5 (step 6 of M4, 35 minutes) and build it in course 6 (step 2 of M5). One real change, and one sentence on an option you rejected. No work outside the course hours.

Every group builds this standard package, whatever their idea.
You built each piece in a milestone, and check.sh M2 to M5 test exactly these lines, nothing hidden.

Your request takes one road: the browser hits the load balancer, the only public door, which forwards to one of your containers in two private subnets, one per AZ. Only those containers can reach the database.
If a container dies, the load balancer stops sending it traffic and ECS starts a new one. Your alarm watches a number, and when it trips your pager gets a message and, for the load alarm, your autoscaler adds a container.

Key point: if something here is missing, say so in your defense.
A gap you name is a decision. A gap I find myself is a surprise, and nobody likes those in production.

⭐ Extras that make your platform stand out

Do these only once the package works.
One extra done well beats three half-done.
Extras earn no points on their own, they make your idea and your answers stronger.

  • A Lambda function triggered by an event, for example a new CV in S3 posts a "new CV" message to your pager.
  • CloudFront caching rules chosen on purpose:
  • a long TTL for the website files
  • CloudFront in front of your load balancer too, caching your app's reads for a few seconds or not at all, depending on your idea
  • A second alarm on what your users care about, wired to your pager.
  • A monthly cost estimate from the AWS Pricing Calculator, link or screenshot attached.
  • A disaster recovery plan in 5 lines: what you back up, where the copy lives, how long a restore takes, how much data you could lose, who presses the button.

🎤 The defense: 8 minutes

Your slot is 8 minutes, timed, and the next group plugs in while you answer questions.
I stop you at 2:30 even mid-sentence, so rehearse once with a timer.

TimeWhat
2 min 30Your pitch. All three speak
2 minBreak-fix: I break something, one of you fixes it
3 minOne question each, about a minute per person
30 sSwap

During the questions I ask what happens if this breaks, what it would cost on real AWS and who sees the data.

A pitch that works:

  • 20 seconds: your idea, with numbers (how many users, when, what data).
  • 1 minute: the diagram, following one request from the browser to the database and back.
  • 1 minute: the live demo, ending on the proof your idea works.
  • 10 seconds each: how your platform scales, how it stays available when one AZ goes, and how it recovers after a disaster.

Before and during your slot:

  • Open everything before your turn: the app URL, the status page through CloudFront, NOTES.md, your templates, a terminal with source env.sh done.
  • Plug in, Floci running, checks green, before your slot starts.
  • Show your idea, don't describe it. If it's the midnight deadline, show the schedule and your scaler's log line.
  • Say "I don't know" when you don't, then say how you'd find out. A team that admits a gap is easier to trust than one that bluffs.

🧪 Hands-on: pre-flight check before your defense

10 minutes before your slot, on the machine you'll demo from, with Floci running:

# The app answers through the load balancer

curl.exe -s -o NUL -w '%{http_code}\n' "http://$DNS/"

# Your stacks, and what carries your tag
aws cloudformation describe-stacks --query 'Stacks[].[StackName,StackStatus]' --output table

How you know it works: every check line says PASS, curl prints 200, both stacks read CREATE_COMPLETE or UPDATE_COMPLETE, and the tag list shows your resources.

🧯 If it doesn't work
  • Something is red and you don't know why: ./wipe.sh then ./infra/rebuild.sh. That's what M5 is for, and it's 2 minutes.
  • The app doesn't answer right after a rebuild: containers start about 30 seconds after the stack.
  • A check about NOTES.md fails: the proof (AccessDenied, page, rebuild time, idea) isn't in NOTES.md, or isn't committed.
  • Floci itself is stuck: docker compose restart, wait 20 seconds.

🧯 Survival tips

  • Your repo is your platform. Whatever isn't in infra/ and NOTES.md at the freeze doesn't exist.
  • Rehearse the live rebuild once with a timer before your slot. If it breaks on the day, you show the Rebuilt in N s line from NOTES.md and the check outputs, and explain.
  • Get your idea working end to end early, then polish. A half-built idea is worse than a clear plan.
  • Floci is an emulator: if you hit something that looks like its bug and not yours (the database reachable by the intruder, no CPU metric), name it in your defense and say what real AWS would do. That's a good answer, not an excuse.

Your diagram is on screen during the pitch, nothing to hand in.
Any diagram tool works (draw.io, Excalidraw, a readable whiteboard photo), as long as it shows:

  • the VPC, the subnets and their AZs
  • every service
  • the arrows a request follows

🤔 Think about it

  • Your idea is the midnight deadline. A teammate wants your scaler's MIN at 4 all week "to be safe". What would that cost on real AWS compared with your plan, and is there a case where they're right?
  • For the live room board, you cache the page in CloudFront for 5 seconds. A seat frees up and the board still shows the room as full. Bug or choice, and how do you pick the number of seconds?
  • Your app, database and containers are all down. What can your users still see, and which piece of your platform made that possible?
  • The internship CVs live in eu-west-3 (Paris), with CloudFront in front of your site. Could a CV leave the EU, and how would you check?
  • You say "the database breaks first at 10 times the traffic". What would you look at in CloudWatch to prove it?
💡 Hints
  • Price both options for a month in the Pricing Calculator: 4 containers all the time against 2 containers plus 2 more from 23:20 to 23:42 on deadline day.
  • Who suffers from 5 seconds of old data, and who suffers when 600 phones hit the database every 3 seconds? The cache time is the price of protecting the database.
  • What does CloudFront serve straight from S3, without the API or the database?
  • CloudFront caches only what you allow. Look at cache rules per path and the Cache-Control headers your API sends.
  • Compare RDS CPUUtilization, DatabaseConnections and ReadIOPS with the ALB's TargetResponseTime. Slow responses with calm server CPU point behind the servers.
